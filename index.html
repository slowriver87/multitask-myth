Multitask Myth

Count. Spell. Then do both at once.

About 1 minute

Tap 1 to 12, then spell MULTITASKING, then alternate the two.

How to play

Round 1: tap the numbers 1 to 12 in order. Round 2: spell MULTITASKING on the letter keys. Round 3: alternate them, 1, M, 2, U, 3, L, all the way to 12, G.

Go as fast as you can. Wrong keys flash red and you try again. Round 3 is the same 24 taps as rounds 1 and 2 combined, so any extra time per tap comes from switching.

To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second. On a computer, press Esc twice.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures how much slower you get when you have to keep switching between two simple tasks. Rounds 1 and 2 give your speed on each task alone. Round 3 makes you switch on every tap. The score is how much longer each tap takes in round 3, as a percentage of your speed in rounds 1 and 2.

How this compares to the lab. Researchers separate two costs [3]. A "mixing cost" compares a block where tasks are mixed with blocks of one task alone. A "switch cost" compares switch trials with repeat trials inside a mixed block. Round 3 switches on every tap, so this game measures both together. It can't split them. The scoring leaves out the first tap of each round, because that tap includes moving your thumb from the Start button.

What the research says

Switching slows you down. People respond more slowly, and usually make more errors, right after a task switch [2].

Preparing helps, but not fully. When people had more time to prepare for a known switch, the cost shrank. But even with 1.2 seconds to prepare, a large cost remained [1].

The old task lingers. In a 1994 study by Allport and colleagues, switching back to reading words after naming ink colors cost more than the reverse. They called the leftover pull of the old task "task-set inertia." A later review describes these findings [3].

Going back to a task you just left is extra slow. People were slower to return to a task they had recently abandoned [4].

Bilinguals show it too. Switching into a person's stronger first language cost more than switching into their weaker second language [5]. This game checks the same idea: counting is a stronger habit than spelling.

People can't judge their own multitasking. How good people thought they were at multitasking had no significant link to how good they actually were. People who multitasked more with media, or used a phone while driving, tended to score worse on a real multitasking test [6].

It follows you to the next task. When people moved on from an unfinished task, part of their attention stayed behind, and their next task suffered [7].

Doctors use a version of this. The clinical Trail Making Test has a simple part and an alternating part. Switching costs measured in the lab were linked to the alternating part, especially to the ratio of the two parts [8].

Where the myth has limits

Adult norms

No published adult norm exists for this exact game. The closest clinical test, the Trail Making Test, is done with pen and paper and scored as a ratio of two completion times. In one study, switching costs were especially large in people whose alternating part took more than 3 times as long as the simple part [8]. Those numbers don't transfer to tapping a keypad, so this game doesn't compare you to a norm.

What's still debated

Researchers agree that switching has a real cost. They still debate how much of it comes from setting up the new task and how much comes from the old task interfering [3].

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. In our simulation of 4,000 rounds by players with a known switching cost, a single round landed within about points of the true value 95% of the time. Pooling 10 rounds narrows that to about ±15 points. That's why your settled score waits for 10 rounds.

Practice helps round 3. Rounds 1 and 2 warm you up on both sequences, which makes round 3 a little easier than it would be cold. So the score may read slightly low.

Phones add noise. Touch screens add a little timing delay, and the letter keys take longer to find than the number keys.

The simulation is a model. The noise levels in it are our assumptions about typical tapping, not measured data. Your own consistency, shown once you settle, is the better guide for you.
